Water Clocks of Medieval Morocco: When Water Became Time

From tanast or tatast in Sijilmasa and Dra'a to the kharūba of Figuig and the manjāna of Marrakech: how Moroccans turned flowing water into measured time and divisible shares — and what that says about a civilisation of water.

1. Water before the clock: why a settled society needed time

Water is not only a resource; it is the condition of settlement. The choice of a town's site turns on the volume of fresh water, the temperateness of the place, the quality of air, and the proximity of pasture and fuel. Where those conditions tighten, water becomes at once a question of survival and a question of social and political organisation.

The source deck reads this relation through the Qur'anic image of water poured down, the earth split open, and food brought forth. Human beings live not on water alone but on water ordered: a measure divided, a span observed, a right discharged. Between a shifting resource and a settled community there had to be an intermediary.

That intermediary was the water clock. Alongside khaṭṭāra galleries, running channels, cisterns and reservoirs, there appeared a quieter technique with a deeper effect: one that converted the flow of water into a unit of time, and made words such as nawba, ḥiṣṣa and fardiyya capable of being regulated.

The water clock is not a subject in itself. It is a medium through which other things are expressed: justice in division, the hierarchy of rights, the times of prayer, and the capacity of a community to govern itself.

2. From Ctesibius and Archimedes to Ibn al-Sa'ati and al-Jazari

The earliest notices of the technique in the deck go back to Greek and Roman writers. Ctesibius worked in Alexandria around the third century BCE; a treatise on the construction of water clocks was attributed to Archimedes; Philo of Byzantium and Vitruvius described later models through which the idea developed.

In the Islamic East, water clocks were described in Damascus and Baghdad and attached to names such as Ibn al-Sa'ati and al-Jazari. Books were composed on the science of clocks and their use, and instruments — the astrolabe, the sine quadrant, the altitude quadrant, the sphere — took their place in the regulation of time. Contacts between the two shores of the Mediterranean carried news of these devices westwards.

Ibn Khaldun supplies the theoretical frame: engineering is the consideration of magnitudes in the absolute, whether line, surface or body. A water clock, in this sense, is an application of a science that governs measure — a measure of water, a measure of time, and a measure of right belonging to the holder of a share.

3. The kharūba: a clock that sinks

In the oasis of Figuig a time-unit is still in use. A semicircular copper vessel with a hole in its base is set in a small cistern or earthen jar filled with water. Water enters through the hole; when the vessel is full it sinks to the bottom, registering one complete unit of measurement.

Inside the vessel are carefully calculated circles or marks, each indicating a fraction of the base unit called the kharūba: a half, a third, a quarter, a fifth, a sixth, an eighth. In other oases, such as Tūlka and al-Aghwāt, a counterpart appears under the name mashkūda.

4. Four meanings: structural, social, religious, economic

The water clock carries four overlapping meanings. It is structural: it binds water, time and space into a single system, making technique the nucleus of a wider order. It is social: it fixes the hierarchy of shares, with the arbāb al-māʾ holding a clearly defined portion; as the number of shares shrinks, those with less must rent them.

Its religious meaning lies in its bond to the times of prayer: shares were reckoned from mid-afternoon to sunset to nightfall, and consultative assemblies met in mosques or after prayer to discuss turns, sales and purchases. Its economic meaning appears when ownership of water becomes more consequential than ownership of land itself — of what use is a field without turns of irrigation? Shares were therefore subject to a distinct fiscal regime.

5. Clocks of the cities: Marrakech and Fez

In Marrakech the name manjāna is attached to the gate of the Kutubiyya mosque: an enormous mechanical clock, fifty cubits high, with bells that sounded every hour. A caution is needed here. According to the deck the manjāna was not a water clock but an automaton — yet it witnesses the same concern with the regulation of time in the Almohad capital. It was mentioned by al-'Umari in Masālik al-abṣār and transmitted by Ibn Sa'id.

Fez, for its part, knew a succession of water clocks mounted on the minaret of the Qarawiyyin mosque. Ibn al-Ḥabbāk set up his clock in the mid-seventh century AH, placing at water level a copper channel with lines that gave signals in cloudy weather, so that people followed his indications by night and day. The Ṣanhājī had a clock whose lines were drawn by 'Abd Allah ibn Warashṭūnī. The deck also names the clock of Abu 'Inan al-Marini, created in 749 AH; the clock of Ibn al-'Arabi, renewed by the jurist Abu 'Abd Allah Muhammad ibn al-'Arabi; and a combination of night-and-day timekeeping with the astrolabe.

6. An oral court in Valencia: water justice at the church door

The deck relates, on the authority of Idris al-Dahhak, an Arab-Islamic popular court that continued in Valencia from 1069. It met at the church door after Friday prayer and consisted of eight elected members representing the owners of the lands watered by eight branches of a single main channel.

They sat in black linen robes; pleading was oral, in the presence of the parties, with no written procedure. Judgment was delivered from the bench before the spectators in the square, and executed with the same speed, without interference by any authority and without compulsion other than the judgment of the crowd. It is a living image of what the management of water can mean when it becomes a binding custom and a continuous popular jurisdiction.

7. From traditional to 'modern': why the technique receded

The deck distinguishes two forms: a traditional form tied to collective use of water, as in the kharūba and mashkūda, and a more complex 'modern' form represented by al-Jazari's clock and the clocks of mosques and schools. The apparent simplicity of these devices should not hide the fact that a number of ideas serving practical ends had a far-reaching effect on the development of the technologies human beings devised.

Today one speaks of a technique whose traditional form is dying out and being replaced by modern ones. The deck concludes that it is not enough to describe such techniques from the outside: their depths must be sounded through archaeological research and an openness to the sciences of water, joining aesthetic form to function, and geographical and historical determinants to both.

8. A Khaldunian close: water management follows civilisation

The deck closes with Ibn Khaldun, who bound water to civilisation and held that the management of water is among the consequences of civilisation, by way of example rather than exhaustiveness. The civilisation of luxury, in his terms, is among the active factors in the social life of middle-sized cities: the rootedness of crafts in towns comes only with the rootedness of civilisation. In both the urban and the rural sphere, water plays a central role in shaping the civilisational character of a country.

That is precisely what the water clock tells us: turning water into divided time was no technical luxury. It was one of the ways a medieval society expressed its justice, its hierarchy, its worship and its economy. Restoring this part of the heritage begins, as the deck sees it, by opening the door to researchers and historians so that they may continue to sound its depths.
